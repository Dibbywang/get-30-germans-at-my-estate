1. Introduction to Electrophysiology

Electrophysiology is the study of the electrical properties of biological cells and tissues. It measures electrical activity and potentials inside living organisms. [00:00]

Key clinical diagnostics include:

  • EEG (Electroencephalogram): Measures electrical fields generated by populations of neurons under the skull. Brain wave frequencies vary depending on state: high-frequency waves occur during perception, memorization, and active concentration, while low-frequency waves occur during sleep or rest. Used to diagnose epilepsy, brain damage, and sleep disorders. [01:21]
  • ECG (Electrocardiogram): Measures the electrical oscillations of the heart beating and pumping blood using 10 electrodes placed on the chest and limbs. [03:41]
    • P wave: Atrial depolarization, initiated at the sinoatrial node, triggering atrial contraction. [05:56]
    • PQ interval: Time difference between atrial and ventricular depolarization. [07:05]
    • QRS complex: Ventricular depolarization. Ventricles are large muscle chambers, hence they generate the strongest electrical signal. [07:05]
    • T wave: Ventricular repolarization. [07:05]
  • EMG (Electromyogram): Measures the electrical activity generated by skeletal muscles.

2. Ion Concentrations & Electrochemical Gradients

Cells maintain an asymmetric distribution of ions across the plasma membrane, creating concentration gradients: [16:10]

Ion Species Extracellular Conc. Intracellular Conc. Typical Behavior
Sodium (Na+) ~145 mM (High) ~15 mM (Low) Influx (Inward current)
Potassium (K+) ~5 mM (Low) ~140 mM (High) Efflux (Outward current)
Chloride (Cl-) ~110 mM (High) ~10 mM (Low) Influx (Hyperpolarizing)
Calcium (Ca2+) ~1-2 mM (High) ~100 nM (Extremely Low) Influx (Signaling & contraction)

This asymmetric distribution separates charge across the insulator phospholipid bilayer. The resting membrane potential inside the cell is slightly negative relative to the outside, typically measuring around -60 mV to -80 mV. [17:13]

3. Active Transport: The Na+/K+ Pump

To preserve concentration gradients, the cell relies on active transport proteins that move ions against their electrochemical gradients using ATP energy. [20:05]

The Sodium-Potassium Pump (Na+/K+-ATPase):

  • Hydrolyzes 1 ATP molecule to pump 3 Na+ ions OUT of the cell and 2 K+ ions IN. [22:33]
  • Because it moves 3 positive charges out and only 2 positive charges in, the pump is electrogenic and contributes directly to the negative resting membrane potential. [22:33]

4. Biophysical Equations & Selectivity

Nernst Equation: Calculates the equilibrium potential (Eion) for a single ion species: [23:56]

$$E_{ion} = \frac{RT}{zF} \ln \frac{[Ion]_{out}}{[Ion]_{in}}$$

At physiological temperatures, this determines the exact voltage at which net flux of that ion is zero (reversal potential). Electrophysiologists use this to establish selectivity by comparing the experimentally measured reversal potential to the theoretical Nernst potential. [25:39]

Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz (GHK) Equation: Calculates the resting membrane potential (Vm) when multiple ions are permeable, weighting their concentrations by relative membrane permeabilities (P): [30:42]

$$V_m = \frac{RT}{F} \ln \frac{P_{K}[K^+]_{out} + P_{Na}[Na^+]_{out} + P_{Cl}[Cl^-]_{in}}{P_{K}[K^+]_{in} + P_{Na}[Na^+]_{in} + P_{Cl}[Cl^-]_{out}}$$

Because the resting membrane is highly permeable to K+ (via open leak channels) and poorly permeable to Na+, Vm is close to EK. The system is in a dynamic homeostatic equilibrium requiring constant pump activity to maintain gradients despite leaks. [32:11]

5. Gating Conformations of Ion Channels

Ion channels are membrane proteins that transition between three primary states to control ionic currents: [41:54]

  1. Closed (Resting): The channel pore is shut. It is closed but responsive to stimulus.
  2. Open (Activated): The stimulus (e.g., depolarization) causes structural shifting of transmembrane helices (voltage sensor), opening the pore to allow passive ion flow.
  3. Inactivated: The channel is still structurally open but blocked by a tethered inactivation domain (e.g., "ball-and-chain" model). Ions cannot pass. The channel must repolarize back to the closed state before it can open again. Inactivation ensures rapid repolarization of action potentials and prevents backward propagation of electrical signals. [43:04]

6. The Patch-Clamp Technique

Developed by Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann in 1976 (Nobel Prize in 1991). A glass microelectrode is pressed against the cell membrane, and suction is applied to establish a Giga-seal (resistance >10 GΩ) to minimize electrical noise. [63:36]

Common Configurations: [65:18]

  • Cell-attached: Pipette seals on intact cell. Measures single-channel activity in the patched patch.
  • Whole-cell: Increased suction ruptures the membrane patch, establishing electrical continuity. Measures sum current of all channels in the membrane.
  • Inside-out: Excising a cell-attached patch by pulling the pipette. The cytosolic side of the patch faces the external bath, allowing researchers to study intracellular gating factors.
  • Outside-out: Excising a whole-cell patch. The membrane fragments reseal, leaving the extracellular face exposed to the bath.
