1. Molecular Adaptations in the Skate BK Channel

Building on the CaV1.3 adaptation, the skate's BK channel also shows crucial evolutionary changes compared to the mammalian (mouse) BK channel. [08:07]

  • In single-channel recordings using the inside-out patch-clamp configuration (which exposes the intracellular side to the bath to control calcium), the skate BK channel showed a much smaller single-channel conductance (< 10 pS) than the mouse (~20 pS). [15:01]
  • However, the skate BK channel stays open for a longer duration (mean open time ~5 ms vs ~1 ms). [16:19]
  • This is due to the loss of negative charges in the S6 pore-lining domain, reducing electrostatic attraction for K+ and lowering conductance while maintaining robust calcium-dependent gating. [19:16]

Together with the window current from CaV1.3, these adaptations drive the stable, self-sustaining membrane potential oscillations essential for detecting minute electric fields. [24:24]

2. Ion Selectivity and the Nernst Equation

To determine what ion a channel transports, electrophysiologists rely on the reversal potential from I-V curves. [34:23]

By shifting the extracellular concentration of an ion and re-measuring the reversal potential, they check if the shift matches the theoretical value calculated by the Nernst Equation. If the experimental data fits the theoretical curve, the channel is highly selective for that specific ion. [37:11]

3. Sensory Biology of Taste

The tongue detects five basic tastes: Sweet, Sour, Bitter, Salty, and Umami (Savory). [41:59]

  • Sweet, Umami, and Bitter use GPCRs (G-protein coupled receptors) to initiate an intracellular signaling cascade ending in calcium release and TRPM5 channel activation. [45:35]
  • Salty taste relies primarily on ENaC (Epithelial Sodium Channels). [47:17]

4. The Quest for the Sour Taste Receptor

Sour taste is the detection of protons (H+) from acidic foods. The molecular identity of the sour receptor was highly controversial. [47:17]

Initially, PKD2L1 (a TRP channel) was widely accepted as the sour receptor based on genetic and pharmacological data (2006). However, by 2011, double-knockout mice lacking PKD2L1 still showed robust nerve responses to acids, meaning the true receptor was still unknown. [55:51]

In 2010, the Liman lab successfully recorded a massive, zinc-sensitive proton current in fluorescently-labeled sour taste cells, establishing the biophysical signature of the missing receptor. [59:32]

5. Discovery of Otop1 as the Proton Channel

After an 8-year search using single-cell RNA sequencing (comparing sour cells to other taste cells), researchers pinpointed Otop1 (Otopetrin-1) as the elusive sour receptor. [65:58]

Otop1 was previously linked to inner-ear gravity sensing (otoconia formation) but its channel function was unknown. [73:30]

Validation of Otop1:

  • Electrophysiology in Xenopus Oocytes & HEK cells: Otop1 expression yielded a robust inward current upon extracellular acidification, which was potently blocked by zinc. [74:39]
  • Selectivity: The reversal potential perfectly tracked the Nernst equilibrium potential for protons across varying pH levels. Introducing high concentrations of competing ions (Na+, K+, Ca2+, Cl-) did not alter the current, proving it is highly selective for H+. [83:09]
  • In Vivo: Otop1 knockout mice completely lost the ability to detect sour tastes, confirming it as the primary mammalian sour receptor. [88:01]
